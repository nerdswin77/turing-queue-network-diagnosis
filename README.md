## What this models

The setting is an operations audit of a service network: a call centre with skill groups and overflow paths, an emergency department with its wards and referral steps, or a fulfilment centre with its work stations. Planners keep a stored model of it (staffing, service rates, buffer sizes, routing tables, a planned arrival profile for the day) and a few monitors report what actually happened in each hour. The real day never matches the plan exactly. Demand in each hour differs from the forecast, the staff work faster or slower than the stored rates, and a handful of things are broken: a station is short of staff, one unit works slowly, a buffer has been closed off, a routing rule sends jobs somewhere else, and a monitor has reported nonsense. The job is to recover all of that from the monitor readings alone. The fault load is realistic: 2 to 5 faults in a network of 20 to 28 stations (a median of 13 percent of the stations, at most 20 percent), so most stations are healthy, and the hard part is telling a local fault from a network-wide shift and one kind of fault from another.

## Difficulty

Each network is a queueing network with limited room at every station. What makes the diagnosis hard is measurement science, not the number of faults.

First, the level of the data is unknown. The demand factor of each period and the service factor of the day are not recorded, so the readings of a healthy station already differ from the stored model, and every fault has to be separated from these two network-wide shifts. A service factor and a slowdown both lower occupancy; the service factor moves every station by the same ratio, a slowdown moves one, and only the healthy stations pin the global factors down. A demand factor and a buffer cut both change what is lost; the demand factor changes what is offered everywhere in that period, a buffer cut the loss of one station in every period.

Second, the fault kinds imitate one another. Losing one of four servers and a slowdown to 0.75 give the same capacity, and only the shape of the occupancy curve over the six arrival levels tells them apart. A smaller buffer raises the lost rate, but only once the station fills, so it shows at the heavy levels and hides at the light ones. A misrouted link sends load to a different part of the network, and its own dispatch meter can't show it, because that meter counts jobs where they leave; the misroute is seen only through the station that unexpectedly receives the load and the one that doesn't.

Third, the meters are sparse: 60 percent of the stations have an occupancy meter, 20 percent an offered meter, 20 percent a lost meter, and 30 percent of the links a dispatch meter. Many faults are seen only through their effect on neighbours, and a gross reading (12 to 30 sigma off) looks like a fault at a neighbouring station unless it is tested against the whole fit. The answer must be exactly right on 18 private networks, including every demand factor within 0.05 and the service factor within 0.01, so one wrong class, one wrong cut or one missed gross reading fails the run.

A default model also goes wrong in two places that the statement spells out: an infinite-buffer product-form network has no losses or blocking, and a dispatch meter doesn't measure what arrives at the link's destination.

## Reference solution

`solution/solve.sh` puts `solution/solver.py` at `/root/results/solver.py`, runs it on the development campaign and compares the result with `environment/data/development_key.json`. No answer is copied in; the solver computes it.

For each network the solver evaluates the reduced-load model for all six periods at once with a batched Newton solve, so many candidate configurations are checked in one call, and it fits the readings with a Huber loss (cutoff 3). The demand factors of the six periods and the service factor are continuous parameters of every candidate configuration: they are refitted jointly with the slowdown factors by Gauss-Newton after every change of the discrete errors, starting from the fault-free network, so each candidate is judged after the global shifts have absorbed what they can. A beam search of width 12 adds one error at a time, trying every station or link, kind and value, up to the statement's limits of 5 errors and 2 gross readings; each error costs 40 and each flagged gross reading 25. The best set is improved by swapping values, dropping each error and retrying every kind at that station, permuting misroute destinations, repairing misroutes jointly, pair moves and relinking; a polish step removes errors that only explain a single outlier. A chi-square statistic of the final fit (limit 2.5) says whether the diagnosis is consistent with the noise; if it isn't, the search restarts from randomised beams with fixed seeds and then from a wider beam, within 240 seconds per network and 620 per campaign. Two processes share the six networks.

The solution doesn't produce the truth. `tests/provenance/generate_data.py` draws the errors, the demand and service factors and the gross readings, runs the independent model in `tests/provenance/qn_physics.py` and writes the readings and `tests/cases/*/truth.json`. Running it again gives every shipped data file back byte for byte.

## Verification

The verifier runs in a separate environment with no network. It holds three private campaigns of six networks each, with their truth, as checksummed archives (`tests/cases/H*/H*_input.tar.gz`). The isolation setup (unprivileged solver account, read-only staged inputs, resource limits, an isolation probe) is adapted from the `navigation-sensor-calibration` task in this repository (Apache License 2.0). The rest is specific to this task. The solver runs once per campaign with the command from the instruction, a 900 second wall-clock limit, 2 CPUs, 4 GB, and an output directory as its only writable place. The exception is `/dev/shm`, which the verifier watches with inotify from before the solver starts until after it exits. Names are matched in full, and more than 1000 created entries fail the run, so a flood of events can't hide a name. The solver starts with hash seed 0, one thread for numerical libraries and the global random generators seeded with 0. A start-up hook, which also reaches worker processes, makes any import outside the standard library, numpy and scipy fail, because the verifier image holds pytest as well. Eight tests per campaign check the schema (value ranges, IDs, structure rules) and the seven answer groups: servers out of service, slowdown factors (within 0.03), buffer cuts, misroutes, demand factors (within 0.05), the service factor (within 0.01) and bad readings.

The 18 private networks have 20 to 28 stations, 29 to 44 links and 29 to 42 meters, and 2 to 5 errors each. Together they hold 4 servers-out entries, 18 slowdowns, 9 buffer cuts, 25 misrouted links and 15 gross readings, and every network has its own demand factors and service factor. The reference gets all of them exactly right. A campaign takes about 4 to 5 minutes. The whole verifier run took 855 seconds while a second job shared the two CPUs.

The generation rules are all structural, and none of them calls the reference. Every margin is measured after the continuous parameters of the alternative (its slowdown factors, the six demand factors and the service factor) have been refitted, so a global shift never hides a fault. Removing any error must raise the fit cost by at least 200. Replacing one error with another, or swapping the values of two errors of the same kind, must leave a cost of at least 100. Every slowdown factor has a Fisher standard deviation of at most 0.008, every demand factor at most 0.01 and the service factor at most 0.002, computed from the full Fisher matrix of all continuous parameters. Every station is offered at least 0.5 jobs per minute. The origin, target and actual destination of misrouted links are all different stations. Ordinary readings stay within 4.5 sigma. The tolerances (0.03, 0.05 and 0.01) are about four to five times the corresponding Fisher limits.
